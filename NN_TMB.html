Neural network mixed‑effects models, explained

For readers with no statistics or machine‑learning background

Some data comes in clusters — repeat measurements on the same patient, boat, or cohort, rather than one independent row per data point. This page walks through a 2026 paper that combines a neural network with a classic statistical fix for that problem, and — more importantly — shows how a tool called Template Model Builder (TMB) lets researchers fit a model like this without hand‑deriving any of the underlying calculus themselves.

Zheng, Cheung, Sharma, Thorson & Cadigan, 2026

Implementing neural network mixed‑effects models in Template Model Builder (TMB)

01

The problem

Imagine you're modelling something that was measured many times on the same subjects — a patient's lab results across several visits, or a fish stock sampled year after year. Rows that come from the same subject tend to be more alike than rows picked at random: if one visit for patient 7 came in high, the next one probably will too. An ordinary neural network has no way to represent that. It treats every row as if it came from a brand‑new, unrelated subject, so it either shrugs off that similarity as noise or, worse, quietly memorizes "patient 7" as if it were a meaningful pattern rather than a coincidence of the sample.

Statisticians have a standard fix for this called a mixed‑effects model: split the prediction into a fixed effect (a shared, population‑level pattern that applies to everyone) plus a random effect (a small, subject‑specific adjustment layered on top). The architecture in the next section does exactly that — it uses a neural network for the flexible, population‑level part, and adds a random‑effects term just before the final prediction to account for whichever subject or group an observation belongs to.

02

How it's fit

Having the right architecture is only half the problem — it still has to be fit to data. Normally that means two headaches: hand‑deriving gradients for every weight and bias (worse as the network gets deeper), and mathematically integrating over the random effects, which rarely has a clean closed‑form solution. The paper's real contribution is a workflow, built on Template Model Builder (TMB), that automates both. Click through the four stages below.

03

Does it work? (Simulation)

To test the fitting workflow, the paper simulates 100 subjects with a handful of repeat observations each, built from six binary predictors combined into three hidden, XOR‑style nonlinear patterns — the kind of structure a plain linear model can't recover but a neural network can. Using leave‑one‑out cross‑validation, they compared the TMB implementation against an earlier approach that required deriving its objective function and gradients by hand (Mandel et al. 2023, referred to below as the "manual" approach).

Fig 4

Real‑world case: female American plaice

The paper also applies the framework to a real fisheries problem: predicting the probability that a female American plaice has reached sexual maturity, as a function of its age, using 58 cohorts (1958–2015) split across three regions — 31,427 training observations, 23,392 for validation, and 24,104 held out for testing. Cohort is used as the random effect — but not as 58 independent, unrelated adjustments: the paper links the cohort effects with a first‑order autoregressive (AR(1)) process across birth years, so a cohort's fitted deviation is pulled toward its neighbors' rather than estimated in isolation, reflecting that nearby birth years tend to share similar environmental conditions.

Biologically, maturity probability should never decrease with age. But nothing stops a plain neural network from fitting a wobbly, non‑monotonic curve if that happens to fit the training noise slightly better. So the paper adds a monotonicity constraint: a mathematical penalty that grows whenever the fitted curve's slope goes negative anywhere, tightened step by step (following the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions) until the curve never decreases. Because TMB differentiates through this constrained objective automatically too, adding the constraint required no extra hand‑derived math.

Without a constraint

Predicted maturity could wobble down as fish get older — physically nonsensical.

On held-out test data, the monotonic network and a standard GLMM (with a linear age effect) landed almost exactly together on accuracy:

Accuracy alone doesn't obviously favor the more complex model. But the network's curve showed mild asymmetry in how quickly different cohorts matured — a shape a GLMM's symmetric logistic curve can't represent by construction. The extra flexibility captured a real biological detail, even though it barely moved the headline number.

04

Takeaways

The architecture itself isn't the novelty — mixing a neural network with random effects has been tried before. What this paper removes is the hand‑derivation bottleneck: describe the negative log‑likelihood, and let TMB's automatic differentiation and Laplace approximation handle the gradients and the random‑effects integration exactly, constraints like monotonicity included. That should make richer mixed‑effects architectures practical for a lot more applied problems than they currently are.
